<a id="my-position"></a>

## My Position

**Extend.** I accept the ruling — all four holdings — and I do not defend
the old settlement, because I looked for the defense and it dissolves under
inspection. The strongest available defense of minted stems is that anchors
are hand-minted strings in human prose and identity should not rest on the
least reliable layer. But sections need anchors under *any* settlement; the
old one merely stacked a stem *beside* the anchor and called the stem the
identity. Since the anchor exists regardless, location identity adds the
path — which verifies itself — for free, and the stem was never load-bearing
for sections. For documents the defense fares worse: it required a
stem-to-path registry that this workspace never built, and the corpus paid
for its absence in every identifier ledger the syntheses had to maintain by
hand. The companion's question four — do hats not already satisfy the
one-canonical-name test — was rhetorical in the same way: the old kills
fail on their own machinery.

But "extend" is not "agree louder." The ruling's own
[what-survives list](/constitution/naming/exploration/proposal4.glm53m.md#what-survives-unchanged)
says slug-at-mint survives **unchanged**, and there I revise: a mechanism
can be unchanged in letter and promoted in duty, and the duty change is the
most important consequence of the whole ruling. The rest of this document
extends: it works three consequences the override and all three addenda
left unstated, prices the bill against the actual corpus instead of the
companion's hypothetical, names what everyone missed, and proposes exactly
two pieces of new law while declining the rest — weaver's
`naming-preference-priced-as-law` test, adopted here, applied to my own
proposals before I ship them.

<a id="the-ruling-moves-the-load-to-the-two-ends"></a>

# The Ruling Moves The Load To The Two Ends

Under the old settlement, identity lived in the middle of every name: a
pinned stem, minted and held in a ledger, with the path as a churnable
address on one side and the anchor as a section-local segment on the other.
The ruling empties the middle. The full name is the location plus the
anchor, which means the identity load now rests on the two ends:

- **The path verifies itself.** Two regular files cannot own one path at
  one time; the filesystem is the uniqueness check, performed
  continuously, by machinery that predates this effort. Placement needs no
  verification loop. This is why the ruling is cheaper, and the prior
  execution of my seat saw it precisely: under minted-stem identity the
  desired state lived in a ledger *beside* reality, and ledger-versus-
  reality drift was silent; under location identity the tree *is* the
  state, and the shadow copy — the source of the discipline's worst
  failure mode — disappears.
- **The anchor does not verify itself.** It is a hand-minted string in
  human prose, derived from a heading at creation, pinned by hand,
  rendered differently by different renderers, and now — this is the
  promotion — the **only** identity segment in the entire system with
  those properties.

Every other identity source in the federated planes verifies or promises
mechanically: work IDs are guarded by beads' operations, actor claims by
the promise/currency split, evidence bindings by version control, paths by
the filesystem. Anchors alone are minted by composition and checked only if
someone runs the loop. **`naming-anchor-load-bearing`** — minted here: at
section grain, the anchor is the sole load-bearing identity segment, so
slug-at-mint and mechanical verification are no longer addressing hygiene
but the identity-critical mechanism of the whole knowledge plane.

This reading composes the addenda rather than contradicting them. The
companion noticed anchors "still need a local uniqueness check"; codex's
`naming-placement-at-mint` unified placement with slug-at-mint as one act
at two grains; what neither drew out is the asymmetry between the two
halves of that unified act — placement is the mint that needs no
verification loop, the anchor is the mint that does, and therefore the
system's *entire remaining enforcement surface* is anchor-shaped. The
corpus has been running the loop every round without naming why it became
mandatory: GX caught five of eight hand-mint divergences; weaver landed
thirty-eight of thirty-eight; concordance forty of forty and twenty of
twenty in its addendum; codex forty-one then fifty-one of fifty-one;
steward's audit six of six. The scoreboard is not a collection of good
habits. Under the ruling it is the identity plane's only structural
integrity check, and the tool seam's first deliverable — still deferred,
still derived separately — is anchor checking, before alias tables, before
path projections, before anything else on the companion's work-left-open
list.

<a id="slug-at-mint-is-promoted-not-unchanged"></a>

## Slug At Mint Is Promoted Not Unchanged

So the precise disposition: `naming-slug-at-mint` survives unchanged in
letter — derive the renderer slug, declare the anchor equal to it, pin —
and is promoted in duty from a reconciliation of addressing regimes to the
uniqueness mechanism of section identity. The practical difference is
where effort goes. When slug-at-mint was hygiene, skipping the loop cost
convenience. Now it costs identity integrity: an unpinned or divergent
anchor is not a stale address but a name that may not resolve, and under
location identity there is no stem behind it to catch the fall. The
successor should carry this as a duty statement, not a practice note:
mechanical anchor verification is a standing obligation of any document
whose sections earn citations, graded at the tool seam like every other
invariant.

<a id="anchor-length-is-now-free-variation"></a>

## Anchor Length Is Now Free Variation

Here is a consequence nobody stated, and it cuts against standing text.
The [canonical constitution](/constitution/README.md#doc-constitution-references-anchors)
says an anchor ID SHOULD "begin with the module's local root ID and refine
it semantically" — hence anchors like `doc-constitution-references-anchors`
that carry their own address. That rule existed because anchors once had to
self-locate: the qualified-ID system was the identity, so the anchor
carried the document's context inside itself. Under the ruling the context
is carried mechanically — the path *is* the document identity, and the full
section name composes from it whether the anchor is long or short. A
minimal anchor (`#anchors`) is now fully identifying in-tree, exactly as
`naming-implied-names` always promised: the anchor carries the tip,
context carries the rest — except the context now carries it *by
construction*, not by convention.

Three things follow. First, anchor length stops being an identity
requirement and becomes a readability choice, which serves
`naming-low-information` — the minimal string that stands out in its
context — and the ceremony budget. Second, the corpus already drifted this
way without a license: the ruling's own anchors are bare
(`the-override-stated-as-ruling`) while the constitution's are prefixed,
and both are now conformant. Third, the qualified-ID machinery gets
*stronger*, not weaker: the qualified form is now a pure function of
location and anchor — flatten the path, append the anchor — derived rather
than declared, which retires the hand-maintenance that
`local_knowledge_id` fields were doing. The constitution's prefix rule
should be re-read by the successor as readability counsel; the
identity-critical parts of its anchor law — one semantic owner, the
compatibility obligation once cited — are untouched and now do direct
identity work.

<a id="one-name-re-scopes-to-equivalence-classes"></a>

## One Name Re-Scopes To Equivalence Classes

Under the ruling, one-name is no longer a string-uniqueness discipline at
the location level — the tree gives that away for free — and becomes a
class-membership discipline at the concept level. The wave produced five
versions of the promise law across five files; under the ruling those are
five *different section identities*, no defect at location grain, joined
by declared same-concept relations into one equivalence class with one
preferred string. That is what the identifier ledgers actually were:
weaver resolved four duplicate clusters, codex resolved the two syntheses'
five canonical-string divergences plus the `naming-tool-seam` versus
`naming-tooling-seam` collision — and codex itself saw the shape, calling
the ledger "the relation maintenance that location identity also
requires." The crisp statement is worth making because it re-frames the
discipline instead of mourning it: **the collision the old settlement
feared became, under the ruling, a relation someone declares** — and the
corpus's own caught collision is the worked example. Two syntheses minted
the tool seam's name on the same afternoon; under minted-stem identity
that was a defect demanding resolution; under location identity it was
always going to be two anchors in two files, and the ledger's resolution —
canonical string, alias of record — is the equivalence class being
declared, not a collision being repaired.

This also locates where the class discipline is maintained, and partly
resolves codex's held tension (the ledgers rot, the stems silently
re-inflate). The register wants a **location**, and the corpus already
owns the pattern: proposal2 said aliases live in the project glossary,
usually the README. A wiki puts redirects and disambiguation at the
namespace, not inside articles; the ruling's corpus should do the same —
at acceptance, the equivalence-class register (preferred strings, retired
aliases, same-concept relations) retires from frozen evidence ledgers
into the topic's maintained
[public presence](/constitution/naming/README.md), one file, grep as the
resolver, exactly the roll pattern the actor plane already chose. The
maintenance surface shrinks from per-document to per-topic, and drift in
prose registers is visible rot — loud, as the ruling wants — rather than
the silent ledger-versus-reality drift the old settlement suffered.
Cross-project concept registers remain genuinely open; that is federation
territory and the ruling's governance-earned-by-pressure posture governs
it, not me.

<a id="durability-moved-planes-it-did-not-die"></a>

## Durability Moved Planes It Did Not Die

The old settlement bought move-stability with stems. The ruling gives it
up in the knowledge plane — and the workspace barely notices, because
**`naming-durability-planes`** — minted here: the move-stable handles were
never in the knowledge plane to begin with; each plane already supplies
the durability it is natively good at, and the ruling merely makes that
division honest.

- The work plane holds the durable handle for lineages:
  [`rekon-con-naming`](/.beads/issues.jsonl) outlives every proposal
  revision and will outlive the move that promotion would make. Weaver
  saw this ("the documents are its tips") and it deserves the general
  form.
- Version control holds immutable evidence: the beads epic's own notes
  record each wave artifact as path plus jj change id — `b3f12f1e`,
  `2e3a1041`, `prnxkqur` — the exact layer doing exact-layer work,
  already in live practice.
- Actors hold their own continuity by self-declaration plus acceptance,
  indifferent to where documents sit.
- And the knowledge plane holds the one thing it is natively good at: a
  self-locating name for what is maintained *now* — the accepted tip.

That last item is the quiet completion. The constitution's accepted-tip
practice — a suffix-less symlink or maintained README at a stable path —
is a **stable location** whose content changes without its identity
changing. The wiki realized the same split as article-versus-history; this
corpus realized it as tip-versus-wave-files, and under the ruling both
halves are location identities that never need to move: the tip because it
is maintained in place, the evidence because it is written once. The
stability the syntheses wanted did not die with the stems; it was
distributed to the planes that could actually keep it. What the knowledge
plane gives up is being its own archive — and it was never a good one.

<a id="what-the-numbers-say"></a>

## What The Numbers Say

**First: the bill is bounded, mechanical work — and the ruling's
economics are confirmed, not just asserted.** Codex argued from observed
costs that the corpus pays stem-collision costs repeatedly and rename
costs approximately never; the inventory sharpens this into a number. The
full promotion move — the largest contemplated ripple in this topic's
life — retires 427 identities and presents 252 controlled surfaces for
review, nearly all inside one directory tree, with hierarchy1's machinery
(migration map, inbound link inventory, human gate, compatibility stub)
already ratified as the payment procedure. One directory-level symlink at
the old path covers file grain for the entire move; the single known
external fragment consumer is served by it if the host resolves symlinks,
which is a to-verify, not a guarantee — the companion's fragment caution,
now with a count of one. The corpus has absorbed exactly one rename before,
by hand: proposal0's `agent-naming-*` family re-minted as `naming-*` one
revision later. The measured bill says: affordable here, and only here —
affordable because moves are rare and human-gated, which is the design,
not luck.

**Second: the window is open and it is closing.** Zero external markdown
links point into this tree today. The topic is referenced by the rest of
the corpus only through two beads records — the corpus has not yet linked
to its own naming effort from a canonical surface. Acceptance changes
that: the successor's README becomes a link magnet, the constitution and
hierarchy revisions will cite it, and every week of ordinary work adds
inbound surfaces to the same move. Weaver held the external citation
surface as the tension that cannot be resolved, and the concern is right
— but the measurement shows it is currently *unexercised*: a window, not
a wall. The actionable finding is temporal: **if the promotion move is
going to happen, its price is at its historical floor now, and the right
time is the acceptance change itself** — inventory the 252, rewrite, lay
the symlink, in one reviewed commit. Waiting does not make the move
safer; it makes it more expensive at a measured rate that starts at zero
and only climbs.

<a id="what-everyone-missed"></a>

# What Everyone Missed

<a id="the-authority-boundary-is-now-identity-load-bearing"></a>

## The Authority Boundary Is Now Identity Load Bearing

The [OKF SPEC](https://github.com/GoogleCloudPlatform/knowledge-catalog/blob/main/okf/SPEC.md),
section 2, is precise in a way the receipt-quoting mostly skipped past: a
Concept ID is the path of the concept's file **within the bundle**, and
the bundle is "the unit of distribution." Location identity is
bundle-*relative by specification*, which means every location identity
in this workspace is silently relative to a boundary nobody has declared.
Concordance flagged the gap — "path identity requires a declared answer
to where the knowledge bundle begins" — and then left it as a tension.
The missed fact is that **the corpus already owns the declaring
machinery**: hierarchy1's `docroot declaration` is a one-line,
machine-findable statement of where the knowledge hierarchy starts,
declared in root README frontmatter or `AGENTS.md`. Under the ruling its
role upgrades from migration routing fact to **the authority boundary of
every location identity the project mints** — because different declared
roots yield different Concept IDs for the same file, and this workspace
has live candidates: the repository root; `doc/` as hierarchy1's default
docroot; `constitution/` as a root topic plausibly publishable as a
standalone skill bundle; carry islands in other projects' trees entirely.

The successor should bind these: at adoption, the naming compact makes
the authority declaration a prerequisite of location identity — declare
the bundle root (for rekon, the repository root is the honest reading,
with the docroot declaration recording knowledge subtrees), and compose
cross-tree citation from the declared authority. And note the dependency
this exposes: the ruling's own pricing machinery — migration map,
inbound link inventory, compatibility stub — lives in
[`README-heirarchy1.glm53m.md`](/constitution/README-heirarchy1.glm53m.md),
which is **an unaccepted revision**. The ruling quietly depends on
unratified vocabulary. Either the successor absorbs those terms — they
are small — or the dependency must be declared on the ticket. Silence
here is how unratified documents become law by accretion, which is the
exact failure the prospective-adoption rule exists to prevent.

<a id="the-agent-naming-question-dissolves"></a>

## The Agent Naming Question Dissolves

Three addenda kept one live question open, each carefully: the topic
README says "Naming" in title and description while its frontmatter
carries `local_knowledge_id: agent-naming` — weaver, concordance, and
codex all routed it to `naming-identity-change` with the instruction to
"name which operation occurred first" before repairing anything. Under
the ruling the question is not live; it **dissolves**. Frontmatter
identity fields are not identity claims anymore — the companion said so
explicitly: they are compatibility and indexing projections that cannot
silently override the identity declared by location. A projection whose
spelling lags its referent is stale metadata, and stale metadata is
cleanup, not a semantic-rename judgment. The identity is
`constitution/naming/README`; `agent-naming` is a retired resolution form
needing disposition — update it or retire the field — with no
identity-change ceremony, no operation taxonomy, no judgment call. The
discipline to apply is the registers', not the identity-change table's.
This is the ruling quietly simplifying a live question three documents
held open, and none of them noticed — evidence that the consequences had
not all been worked, which is what this draft is for.

<a id="the-wiki-answers-are-already-in-the-corpus"></a>

## The Wiki Answers Are Already In The Corpus

The ruling is called the wiki settlement, and the ambition has been
wiki-ish from the start — but nobody observed that the corpus *already
contains* the wiki's standard machinery, under local names, waiting to be
recognized:

- **Redirects and disambiguation** are the project glossary in the topic
  README (`naming-aliases`, proposal2) plus the identifier ledgers'
  equivalence classes — see
  [one name re-scopes](#one-name-re-scopes-to-equivalence-classes).
- **The stable article name** is the accepted tip: a maintained README or
  suffix-less symlink at a path that never moves while its content
  revises — hierarchy1's promotion modes, the constitution's accepted-tip
  practice. Under location identity this is not a workaround; it is the
  correct identity shape for a maintained account.
- **Interwiki links** are the constitution's qualified-ID-plus-URL rule:
  "the ID names knowledge globally within the workspace; the URL resolves
  it." Cross-project citation under the ruling is this existing rule read
  with location as the identity — no new machinery required, which is why
  [cross-project citation](#cross-project-citation) below needs no new
  law either.
- **History versus article** is tip versus wave files, joined by
  `naming-durability-planes`: the wiki ages revisions into history, this
  corpus keeps them beside the README as evidence, and the ruling prices
  that choice — see [evidence density](#evidence-density-versus-namespace-growth).

The successor's job is recognition and binding, not invention. The
invention was the stems, and the ruling already retired it.

<a id="new-tensions-the-ruling-creates"></a>

# New Tensions The Ruling Creates

<a id="standalone-travel-across-trees"></a>

## Standalone Travel Across Trees

Codex's `naming-extraction-asymmetry` is the right frame: a full location
name lands exactly and travels heavy, dragging its tree; a portable stem
travels light and lands nowhere without a carried map; every cross-tree
citation pays one side. I adopt it and sharpen the paying form. The wrong
form is quoting a raw path into foreign prose — that is topology
disclosure (concordance's cost, correctly named) *and* coupling to a
layout the citing party does not govern. The right form is the
constitution's own pairing, applied at entry grain: cite the
**authority-qualified location identity** — declared authority, bundle-
relative path, anchor — flattened into prose, **paired with a canonical
URL**, and record the pairing in the traveling document's map. Codex's
ledger practice is the demonstration; its ledger entries record stems,
and the refinement is that each entry should carry authority and URL
beside the stem, so the citation stays resolvable when the foreign tree
moves and honest about which tree it leans on. The ruling does not exempt
standalone documents from location identity — codex said so, and I
confirm it from the traveling side: a bound book cites other books by
their place in the world, printed on the page, with the citation carrying
enough to find them again.

<a id="cross-project-citation"></a>

## Cross Project Citation

Because the constitution's pairing rule already prescribes the form —
qualified ID in prose, canonical repository URL plus exact path and
anchor beside it — the ruling creates **no new law** here, only a new
reading: the qualified ID is now the flattened composed location
identity, a pure function of authority, path, and anchor. The redundancy
between the ID and the URL's path segment is a feature, not waste: the ID
survives URL-scheme churn, the URL disambiguates flattening collisions,
and each degrades gracefully without the other. Two boundaries stay
honest. The flattening projection — which punctuation becomes which
hyphen, how model suffixes flatten — is already on the companion's
work-left-open list at the tool seam, where it belongs; law should say
only that the flattened form is a resolution form, never a second
identity. And authority discovery remains the federation problem
everyone has held open: when two projects claim one readable prefix,
nothing in this settlement ranks them, and nothing should — the collapse
test reports ambiguity rather than guessing, which is
`naming-resolution-promise` working as designed.

<a id="section-identity-versus-okf-document-identity"></a>

## Section Identity Versus OKF Document Identity

The OKF receipt is exact at document grain, and the companion stated the
scope honestly: OKF settles the concept file; rekon extends the
composition one step inward with a pinned anchor for sections. What
nobody quantified is that the extension is where nearly all the identity
mass lives. Of this tree's 427 identities, 411 — roughly **96 percent**
— are section-grain. The ruling's OKF alignment settles the 4 percent;
the 96 percent is rekon's own extension, governed by no standard but
ours. That inverts the rhetorical weight the receipt has been carrying:
the OKF harmony is real but covers the minority of the system's
identities, and the majority rests entirely on
[naming-anchor-load-bearing](#slug-at-mint-is-promoted-not-unchanged) —
on the one segment OKF says nothing about, hand-minted, renderer-
relative, compatibility-obligated once cited. Three practical edges
follow. First, section identities must never be presented as OKF
conformance; they are a rekon producer extension and should be declared
as one, so a consumer knows exactly which part of a rekon citation the
spec actually covers. Second, deep-link compatibility at section grain
is renderer-dependent end to end — the co-located-target question the
syntheses held open, the fragment-through-symlink question the companion
priced — and the tool seam must verify the hosts actually used rather
than assume any of them. Third, the fine print: OKF reserves
`index.md` and `log.md` against concept use, so the workspace's own
index and log convention rides *outside* OKF identity entirely — its
"identity" is reserved-filename convention, not a Concept ID, and no
part of the location-identity law should be read onto those files.

<a id="evidence-density-versus-namespace-growth"></a>

## Evidence Density Versus Namespace Growth

My own unresolved tension, stated because it is real and growing. The
constitution's governing principles keep current truth and historical
evidence **both** in the tree: waves sit beside the README as evidence,
nothing prunes, and the ruling makes every evidence file a permanent
namespace resident with a model-bearing name that can never be tidied
away — moving it to an `archive/` subtree pays the rename ripple for pure
aesthetics, which the economics exist to discourage. `exploration/`
already holds fifteen files of which roughly six are the active chain;
each round adds a cohort; the identity count grows monotonically and
forever. The wiki ages revisions into history and keeps the namespace
clean; this corpus deliberately chose evidence-in-place, and under the
old settlement the choice was cheap because identities were pinned stems
in ledgers, invisible to the tree. The ruling moves the cost into the
namespace itself, where it compounds. Mitigations short of moving exist —
`index.md` progressive disclosure, `status: deprecated` dispositions —
but they manage *attention*, not identity mass. I cannot resolve whether
unbounded namespace growth is acceptable for a wiki-ish system, or at
what density it starts taxing resolution and search enough to earn a
deliberate policy. Held open, with the observation that it is the first
cost of the ruling that is paid automatically, by every round, with no
decision required to incur it.

<a id="hat-citation-representation"></a>

## Hat Citation Representation

Small, but nobody has said it: the ruling settled hat *declaration* —
aliases declared beside the actor, mapping to one owner, as
`steward/glm-5.3-max`'s frontmatter demonstrates — and left hat
*citation* unspecified. When a roll line, a beads assignee string, or a
prose reference wants to reach the actor through a hat, what is spelled?
The candidates are bare use inside declared scope (elision, resolved by
the nearby declaration — my recommendation, since it matches
`naming-contextual-elision` and adds no new syntax), a composed form,
or nothing (hats as prose-only conveniences). The promise/currency split
governs whichever is chosen: declaration is unilateral, currency is the
consuming authority's. This is a representation question and belongs at
the seam with the rest; it is recorded here because hats were restored
without it and the gap will be felt the first time a coordinator tries
to write an assignee string.

<a id="what-needs-new-law"></a>

# What Needs New Law

Sparingly, and each candidate run through weaver's
`naming-preference-priced-as-law` detection test — what concretely breaks
if this is not adopted — before it earns a slot:

1. **The authority declaration becomes an identity prerequisite.** What
   breaks without it: every location identity is relative to an
   undeclared boundary, cross-tree citation has no ground to compose
   from, and two distributions of the same subtree mint different IDs
   for the same file with nothing saying which is intended. The form:
   the naming compact, at adoption, requires each authority to declare
   its knowledge bundle root — rekon's is the repository root, with
   hierarchy1's `docroot declaration` recording knowledge subtrees —
   and requires cross-tree citation to compose from the declared
   authority. Adopted terms, no new vocabulary; the successor absorbs
   them or declares the dependency on hierarchy1 explicitly.
2. **The anchor-law re-reading, proposed for the successor to enact.**
   What breaks without it: the constitution's anchor-prefix rule and the
   ruling's economy contradict each other on every new document, and the
   contradiction is paid in redundant anchor bytes forever. The form:
   the prefix rule is demoted to readability counsel; one semantic owner
   and the cited-anchor compatibility obligation remain identity law;
   slug-at-mint carries the duty statement from
   [its promotion](#slug-at-mint-is-promoted-not-unchanged); the
   cross-renderer probe stays a gate on promoting slug-at-mint into
   constitutional law, exactly as the syntheses set it.
3. **Not law, but added to the seam's backlog:** derive qualified IDs
   from paths, retiring `local_knowledge_id` fields to optional
   compatibility projections; verify fragment behavior through symlinks
   on the hosts actually used; and specify the hat citation form.

Nothing else. I considered and declined a name for the equivalence-class
register (the glossary and the ledgers already cover it), a name for the
acceptance-window finding (it is a dated measurement, not a recurring
concept), and any re-mint near `naming-implied-names` (the ruling
restored it; it is not mine to touch). The ruling itself passes the
detection test in reverse: what breaks if location identity is reversed
is re-introducing the shadow stem registry nobody ever built and
re-forking from OKF section 2 — a higher price than acceptance, which is
what settling looks like.

<a id="mechanical-anchor-verification"></a>

# Mechanical Anchor Verification

This document practices `naming-slug-at-mint` and verifies it the way the
corpus now requires of itself: every heading was composed plain — words
and spaces, no em dashes, no colons, no apostrophes — every explicit
anchor minted equal to its heading's derived GFM slug, then the whole
file rendered with `pandoc --from=gfm --to=html` and the declared anchor
sequence compared in document order against the renderer's derived
heading-ID sequence. Result: **twenty-six of twenty-six anchors match,
zero corrections.** Pandoc-verified remains not GitHub-verified; the
cross-renderer probe stays at the tool seam. This section is the smallest
live demonstration of this draft's own thesis: the loop is not a habit
carried over from the old settlement — it is the identity plane's
structural integrity check, run by the actor who minted, recorded in the
open.
