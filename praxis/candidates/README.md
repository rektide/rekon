# Candidates

**Give the human more than one thing worth wanting.** When an idea has room
for different shapes, develop candidates that make those possibilities real
enough to understand, compare, and pursue. Do not deliver one predetermined
answer wearing three different labels.

The human explicitly wants this practice. The account below is its first
draft, not a mandate to run a multi-agent wave for every small decision.

## Terms in use

- **Candidate:** a possible answer, design, expression, or course being given
  serious consideration. It need not already be the preferred one.
- **Alternative:** another meaningful possibility in relation to a candidate.
  It may serve a different need rather than compete for exactly the same slot.
- **Comparison:** an account of consequential differences, their grounds, and
  where each candidate is attractive or costly.
- **Run-off:** separately developed attempts brought together for comparative
  reading. Independence depends on what each author saw, not a model count.
- **Selection:** choosing what to use or pursue for a stated purpose. It need
  not reject every other candidate or establish that the choice is universally
  best.
- **Synthesis:** a new candidate combining or reconciling elements. It must
  explain what it retains, changes, and loses; it is not automatically superior.

The [shared glossary](/CONTEXT.md) supplies the broader distinctions among
authority, acceptance, checkpoints, evidence, and intent fronts.

## Develop real differences

Start from the human's situation and desired experience. Ask what could vary
meaningfully: who the thing serves, its public interface, where decisions live,
what it makes easy, what it protects, or how it can grow. Do not lock these axes
before hearing the human's framing.

Two or three candidates may be enough. Sometimes several sketches make the
space legible; sometimes one deeply developed candidate plus a genuinely
different counterproposal is more useful. The amount of development should
make the decision possible, not satisfy an arbitrary quota.

Each candidate should get a fair account of:

- the experience or outcome it offers;
- the organizing idea that makes it different;
- a small example showing that difference;
- what supports it and what remains an assumption;
- what becomes easier, harder, possible, or impossible;
- what would make it the attractive choice.

These are questions for writing, not mandatory headings on every sketch.
Names should help a reader remember the candidates' different ideas, not imply
that one is sensible and the others are strawmen.

## Let the candidates change the question

Sometimes the comparison reveals that the initial question was too narrow.
The human may be choosing an experience, not an implementation detail. A
clarification can change the candidate set rather than merely change a score
inside the old set.

For example, an OpenCode outcome gate might be a JavaScript registry with
explicit consumers, a plugin-owned dispatch path, or a host-integrated release
boundary. Those candidates differ in **which flows they can actually govern**.
Calling all three “ways to await a Promise” would conceal the important choice.
The [Openintent README](file:///home/rektide/src/openintent/README.md) holds that
specific idea and its still-unverified integration boundaries.

For a documentation pass, a shared vocabulary-first approach, independent
domain-owned drafts, and encounter-led revisions can all be serious candidates.
Their coordination costs and learning opportunities differ. None requires
deciding the directory tree before understanding the work.

## Compare without flattening

Read the candidates completely enough to explain their arguments. Compare
against the human's purposes, not only the features the candidates happen to
share. Keep source facts, interpretations, preferences, and unknowns distinct.

Useful outcomes include:

- selecting one for this use;
- selecting different candidates for different uses;
- keeping more than one active;
- writing a synthesis that preserves the important differences;
- identifying a small experiment or clarification that would change the choice;
- deciding none yet earns selection.

A recommendation is welcome. Explain it and what it sacrifices. Do not force
a winner just to make the answer look finished, or avoid judgment by giving
an undifferentiated list. Agreement between models may reflect shared inputs
and framing; the [naming override](/constitution/naming/exploration/proposal4.glm53m.md)
is a concrete reminder that convergence can miss what the human wanted.

## Use collaborators deliberately

Multiple models are one way to get candidates, not the definition of this
practice. A parent with the relevant session experience should contribute its
own judgment rather than delegate that experience away.

If independent attempts are wanted, agree what they are trying, preserve their
inputs and owned outputs, and hold comparative reading until the declared
[gate](/assess/gates/README.md) opens. A parent with the full conversation and
a child given a summary do not have identical evidence conditions; their
comparison can still be valuable without calling it a controlled benchmark.

An alternative mode is cooperative development: let people challenge and
improve one another's candidates as they form. That tests dialogue and revision,
not independent discovery. Name which mode is being used rather than treating
one as the only legitimate way to think.

## Keep alternatives without keeping everything in attention

Preserve the candidate and the reason it remains interesting. A short note
about what would make it worth revisiting is more useful than silently losing
it or marking every unselected idea “rejected.”

When the human does not want to lose attention on a possible pursuit, **capture
the intent in a ticket**: their todo log, not a claim that implementation was
approved. Link the candidate and the unresolved choice. Do not automatically
make a ticket for every generated variation. [The intents inquiry](/design/feature-lines/init1.gpt.md#intents)
records this direction and the `rekon-intents` attention reminder.

Part of praxis is also **regathering and focusing**. After an expansive round,
bring the candidates back together, name what we learned, and choose the next
coherent action. Preservation lets the human focus without pretending the
other possibilities never mattered.

## How we will tell whether this helps

The practice helped if the human can see meaningful possibilities they were
not previously being offered, understand the differences, and choose or defer
with better reasons. More candidates, more models, more words, or a completed
comparison table are not themselves the result.

Keep the experiment proportional. Record a useful surprise or a poor comparison
through the existing [case-story practice](/evidence/README.md#evidence-case-stories)
when it teaches something; do not require a report after every choice.
